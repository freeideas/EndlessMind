# Explainer video: the problems Endless Mind solves

A script for one video of about five minutes. It says every word to be spoken and, briefly, what is on screen while it is. Nothing is recorded or rendered until the owner approves the words. The advice it follows is in [VidFact](../../VidFact/specs/making-videos.md) and its [checks](../../VidFact/specs/quality.md).

## The plan

- **Who it is for:** someone who can make a game with an AI coding agent, or can imagine doing it, and has never heard of Endless Mind.
- **The one idea:** you made a game with AI; Endless Mind is how everyone else gets to play it.
- **Order:** five problems, in the order a maker runs into them. Each part opens with the problem in the maker's own words, on screen, and only then shows the fix. Nothing is explained before the viewer knows why they would want it.
- **How each fix is told:** what the maker or player does first (it is short), then one or two sentences on why it works. The machinery is never the subject.
- **Pace:** about 2.8 words a second, with something new on screen every 2 to 4 seconds. The times include short pauses between scenes.
- **The example game:** Lantern Tag, an original game of tag in a dark maze. Each player carries a lantern, and whoever is "it" carries a red one.
- **Sound off:** each problem is a line of text on screen, and each fix has a short line of its own.

| Problem, in the maker's words                               | Fix                                        | Scenes |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------ |
| "I made a game. How does anyone else play it?"              | A link that works anywhere, with no server | 3-7    |
| "Players want to be someone, and nobody wants an account."  | Scan a code, tap once                      | 8-11   |
| "AI makes a million games and fake fans. Who finds mine?"   | Records of real play                       | 12-15  |
| "My game stops when my computer does."                      | Hosts pick up the games people wish for    | 16-20  |
| "A platform could switch my game off."                      | No center                                  | 21-22  |

## Script

### Opening

**1. The problem everyone hits (0:00, 11 s)**

- Show: Lantern Tag running in one window on one desk: lanterns in a dark maze. A message pops up from a friend: "Looks great. How do I play it?" No answer is typed. Text: "I made a game. Now what?"
- Say: "You can make a multi-player game with AI in one evening. Then comes the hard part. The game is on your computer, and your friends are somewhere else."

**2. The name (0:11, 6 s)**

- Show: the single window is joined by friends' windows, all in the same maze. Title: "Endless Mind".
- Say: "Endless Mind solves that problem, and the four that come right after it."

### Problem 1: "How does anyone else play it?"

**3. The usual answer (0:17, 11 s)**

- Show: a to-do list grows down the screen: rent a server, buy a name, set up certificates, learn to deploy, keep it running. The maker's shoulders drop. Text: the problem line.
- Say: "The usual answer is to rent a server, buy a name, set up certificates, and learn to deploy. That is a second project, and it is not the fun one."

**4. Ask your agent instead (0:28, 13 s)**

- Show: the to-do list is swept away. A real coding agent in a terminal, with the request typed out: "Make a game called Lantern Tag for Endless Mind. Players carry lanterns in a dark maze, and whoever is 'it' has a red one. Follow github.com/freeideas/EndlessMind."
- Say: "Instead, ask your AI coding agent, Claude, Codex, Gemini, any of them, to build the game for Endless Mind. Just point it at the repository."

**5. You get a link (0:41, 8 s)**

- Show: the agent prints a link. A browser opens it and one lantern walks the maze. Text: "You get a link."
- Say: "The agent writes the game, starts it on your own computer, and hands you a link."

**6. The link works anywhere (0:49, 19 s)**

- Show: the link is pasted into the chat from scene 1. Friends in other cities open it. A go-between on the web carries their lines to the house. Four lanterns in one maze. Text: "No server. Nothing to install."
- Say: "That link works for anyone, anywhere. A relay, a go-between on the web that the program finds by itself, carries your friends to your computer. You rented nothing. They install nothing. Send the link, and you are all in the same maze."

**7. Playing together is already handled (1:08, 16 s)**

- Show: the game server in the middle of the four players. Five small labels arrive: started, joined, left, message, timer. Messages go back out, and the lanterns stay in step.
- Say: "Keeping several players in step is normally the hard part of a game. Here your agent only has to answer five things: the game started, a player joined, a player left, a message came in, a timer went off. That is enough for tag, for chess, for a whole world."

### Problem 2: "Players want to be someone, and nobody wants another account."

**8. The problem (1:24, 15 s)**

- Show: a player's lantern labeled "Guest" loses its progress when the page closes. Then a sign-up form: email, password, confirm password, "forgot your password?". Text: the problem line.
- Say: "Second problem. People who like your game want to be someone in it: keep their progress, have a name. Normally you would build sign-up, passwords and password resets, and every player would make one more account to forget."

**9. Scan and tap (1:39, 13 s)**

- Show: the sign-up form is replaced by a square code on the game screen. A phone scans it, names the site, one tap. "Guest" becomes "Witty Clover". Text: "Scan. Tap. In."
- Say: "Here, your game shows a code. The player scans it with their phone and taps once. They are in. The first time, the phone sets them up in about a minute. You built nothing."

**10. Why nobody can take it away (1:52, 18 s)**

- Show: the phone, with twenty-four ordinary words shown once and a screenshot taken (a made-up phrase). The words stay inside the outline of the phone; arrows from a game and from a company try to reach them and stop at the edge. Text: "Yours. Not ours. Not the game's."
- Say: "It works because the player's identity is not kept by you, or by us. It lives on their phone, as a secret phrase of twenty-four words that never leaves it. So there is no password to leak, and nobody who can lock them out."

**11. One identity, every game (2:10, 17 s)**

- Show: "Witty Clover" walks from Lantern Tag into three other, different games and is greeted by name in each. Then a lost phone, a screenshot, and a new phone with the same name on it.
- Say: "The same identity works in every game on the network, so a new player arrives in your game with a name and a history, not as a stranger. Lose the phone, and a screenshot of the phrase brings it all back. If it is stolen, burn it and start again."

### Problem 3: "AI makes a million games and a million fake fans. Who finds mine?"

**12. The problem (2:27, 13 s)**

- Show: a shelf of games multiplies until it fills the screen. Under each, rows of identical five-star reviews from identical gray faces. Lantern Tag is lost among them. Text: the problem line.
- Say: "Third problem. AI can make a million games, and a million fake players to praise them. So how does a good game get found? And how does a player show that they are real?"

**13. Records of real play (2:40, 18 s)**

- Show: a game offers a card: "Finished the Glass Maze in 4 minutes 12 seconds." The phone shows the same words. The player approves, and two marks appear: the player's and the game's. Another game checks the card and lights up; a copy with one word changed goes dark. Text: "Proof of play."
- Say: "With records. When a player does something worth remembering, the game offers them a record. Their phone shows the exact words, and if they agree, both sign it. Anyone can check it. Nobody can fake it, or change a word."

**14. A reputation that is yours (2:58, 11 s)**

- Show: the player's shelf of records from many games. They keep most and drop one.
- Say: "Players keep the records they like, so a reputation only grows. It is what you really did, in every game you played, and it goes where you go."

**15. Fake crowds count for nothing (3:09, 19 s)**

- Show: the star reviews and gray faces from scene 12 fade to zero. Light spreads from one trusted game, to the players who really played there, to the next game they spend time in. Lantern Tag rises on a list. Text: "No likes. No votes. Play."
- Say: "Games are found the same way: by real play, not likes or votes. A thousand invented players count for nothing, because a player counts only when a game that is already trusted has their play on record. Trust spreads outward from real play."

### Problem 4: "My game stops when my computer does."

**16. The problem (3:28, 7 s)**

- Show: the maker closes the laptop. In four other windows the lanterns freeze, then go dark. Text: the problem line.
- Say: "Fourth problem. Your game stops when your computer does, and you still do not want to run a server."

**17. Hosts and their boards (3:35, 12 s)**

- Show: several small servers, each owned by a different person. On one, a board: a list of games with how many players have played each.
- Say: "Other people do. Anyone can run a host: the same program, on a small rented server. Every host has a board of the games the network runs."

**18. Wishes (3:47, 14 s)**

- Show: a player taps "wish" on Lantern Tag. The wish count rises on several boards. One host loads the game, then another. Text: "Wanted games find a home."
- Say: "A player who likes your game can wish for it there. Hosts that allow it load the most wished-for games by themselves. So your game finds a home without you asking anyone, or paying anyone."

**19. Why a stranger would run your code (4:01, 12 s)**

- Show: Lantern Tag's game server inside a sealed glass box on a host. It reaches for the files and the network and cannot touch them. Messages to players pass through one small slot.
- Say: "Why would a stranger run your code? Because every game server is sealed. It has no files and no network. All it can do is talk to its players."

**20. The game plays on (4:13, 6 s)**

- Show: the closed laptop from scene 16. The four windows light up again, now connected to hosts.
- Say: "Turn your computer off. The game plays on."

### Problem 5: "A platform could switch my game off."

**21. The problem (4:19, 10 s)**

- Show: a big storefront with Lantern Tag on its shelf. A hand changes a sign on the door: new rules, a bigger cut, then "closed". Text: the problem line.
- Say: "Last problem. Put your game on someone's platform, and they can change the rules, take a cut, or switch it off."

**22. No center (4:29, 17 s)**

- Show: the storefront fades out. What is left is many small hosts, none bigger than the others. Cards pass along the lines between them. One host goes dark, and the rest stay lit and connected.
- Say: "Endless Mind has no center. Hosts find each other by passing along signed cards that say where they are and what they run. Reach one host, and you can find the rest. No host is special, ours included."

### Close

**23. Your turn (4:46, 12 s)**

- Show: the chat from scene 1. This time the maker answers the friend with a link. Text: "endlessmind.com", held for at least four seconds.
- Say: "So describe a game tonight, and send someone the link. The rest is already solved. Endless Mind is free, open, and yours to build on."

## Before filming

The film must show only what really works, on real screens.

- **Lantern Tag has to be real.** Have an agent build it from the request in scene 4, and record that session and the game itself. Scenes 1, 4, 5 and 6 use those recordings, not drawings of them.
- **Scene 4 depends on how the program is installed.** Today that is a checkout of the repository with Deno. If the package is published first ([PLAN.md](../PLAN.md), step 1), the scene can show the one-line install instead.
- **Scene 9** says the first-time setup takes about a minute. Time it with someone new before recording, and change the words to match.
- **Scene 17** is true with the program run from a checkout. `sudo endlessmind setup` has not yet run on a real server (step 2 of the plan), so do not show that command until it has.
- **Scenes 18 and 20** need a second host that loads games by demand, which a host does only when its admin turns that on, and only for wishes from players it has reason to believe. Record them on two real hosts.
- **Scene 10** shows a made-up phrase, and scene 9 a real sign-in recorded with a throwaway identity.
- **Names of AI agents** appear as plain text, without logos.
